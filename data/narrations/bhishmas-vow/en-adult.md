Santanu ruled the Kurus from Hastinapura with such virtue that all the kings of the earth called him King of kings, and under him no creature was needlessly killed, and the kings of the earth themselves lived free of woe and fear. [[mahabharata:adi:100:1]] His son, born of the river-goddess Ganga and named Devavrata, resembled his father in beauty and bearing, and grew into a mighty warrior, skilled in every branch of knowledge. [[mahabharata:adi:100:2]]

One day, while following a deer he had wounded along the bank of the Ganges, Santanu noticed that the river had suddenly grown shallow. Wondering at this, he searched for the cause and came upon a youth of great beauty who had checked the river's flow with a celestial weapon. The king did not recognize that this youth was his own son, whom he had seen only once as an infant, and the young man, though he knew his father at once, veiled himself in illusion and vanished from Santanu's sight rather than reveal himself. [[mahabharata:adi:100:2]]

Astonished, Santanu called on Ganga to show him the child, and Ganga appeared in radiant form, the boy in her arms, and told him, "This is that eighth son I bore you; take him now, for I have reared him with care." She said the boy had studied the whole of the Vedas under Vasishtha, was a mighty bowman favored by gods and Asuras alike, and knew all the weapons that the sage Rama, son of Jamadagni, knew. Santanu took his son home to Hastinapura and, gathering the Paurava chiefs, installed him as his heir. [[mahabharata:adi:100:3]]

Four years passed. Wandering in the woods by the Yamuna one day, Santanu followed a sweet fragrance and found a maiden of celestial beauty, the daughter of the chief of the fishermen, who ferried travelers across the river. Santanu desired her as his wife and went to her father to ask for her. The fisherman answered that he would give his daughter only on one condition: that the son born of her should be installed on the throne, and no other. [[mahabharata:adi:100:4]]

Santanu could not bring himself to grant such a pledge, for it would mean setting aside Devavrata, and he returned to Hastinapura heavy with unspoken sorrow, thinking of the fisherman's daughter all the way. Devavrata, seeing his father grown pale and joyless, asked what illness troubled him. Santanu told him only that he feared for the fragility of life: Devavrata was his one son, brave and always at arms, and should any misfortune take him, the whole dynasty would perish with him, for a king who has but a single son has, as the wise say, no son at all. [[mahabharata:adi:100:5]]

Devavrata thought this over, then went to an old minister devoted to his father's welfare and learned the truth: it was the fisherman's demand about his daughter's son that had brought the king low. Devavrata, with a company of elder chiefs, went himself to the fisherman and asked for the maiden's hand on his father's behalf. The fisherman received him with honor, saying that even if he himself were as great as Indra, he would regret refusing so honorable a proposal — yet he had one objection: whoever had Devavrata as a rival, he said, had no security, be that rival Asura or Gandharva. [[mahabharata:adi:100:6]]

Devavrata answered at once, before the assembled chiefs: "The son born of this maiden shall be our king." The fisherman, still uneasy, said he did not doubt Devavrata's own word, but he doubted what children Devavrata himself might one day beget. Hearing this, Devavrata declared, "From this day I adopt the vow of lifelong celibacy. Sonless though I remain, I shall still win the regions of everlasting bliss." At these words the fisherman's hair stood on end with joy, and he cried, "I bestow my daughter!" The Apsaras and the gods rained flowers from the sky upon Devavrata's head and called out, "This one is Bhishma, the terrible!" [[mahabharata:adi:100:7]]

Bhishma then brought the maiden to his own chariot, addressed her as mother, and carried her home to Hastinapura, where he told Santanu all that had happened. The assembled kings praised his extraordinary vow, and Santanu, overjoyed, granted his son a boon: that death would never come to him until he himself willed it, but would first have to await his command. [[mahabharata:adi:100:7]]
