Long ago, a good king named Santanu ruled the Kurus from his city of Hastinapura, and all the other kings called him King of kings because he was so fair and kind. [[mahabharata:adi:100:1]] His son Devavrata, born of the river-goddess Ganga, grew up to be strong and wise, skilled with every weapon. [[mahabharata:adi:100:2]]

One day, while Santanu was walking by the Ganges, he saw the river had grown strangely shallow. He looked for the reason and found a handsome young man who had stopped the river's flow with a magical weapon. The young man was really his own son, but Santanu did not know him, since he had only seen him as a baby. And the young man, though he recognized his father at once, hid himself with magic and disappeared instead of saying who he was. [[mahabharata:adi:100:2]]

Puzzled, Santanu asked Ganga to show him the boy. Ganga appeared, holding the child, and said, "This is the son I bore you. I have raised him well. Take him home." She told Santanu the boy had studied with the sage Vasishtha and had learned to use every weapon there was. Santanu happily took his son home and made him the heir to his throne. [[mahabharata:adi:100:3]]

Four years later, Santanu was walking near the river Yamuna when he smelled a lovely scent. He found a beautiful girl, the daughter of the chief of the fishermen, who rowed people across the river. Santanu fell in love with her and asked her father if he could marry her. The fisherman said he would agree only if Santanu promised that her son, and no one else, would become king one day. [[mahabharata:adi:100:4]]

Santanu could not make that promise, because it would mean Devavrata could never be king. He went home very sad and would not say why. Devavrata noticed his father looked pale and unhappy and asked what was wrong. Santanu told him only that he worried about what might happen to his kingdom if anything ever happened to Devavrata, his only son. [[mahabharata:adi:100:5]]

Devavrata asked an old minister what was really troubling his father, and learned about the fisherman's demand. So Devavrata went to the fisherman himself and asked for his daughter's hand on his father's behalf. The fisherman welcomed him kindly, but said he had one worry: Devavrata himself was so strong and mighty that no one whose rival was Devavrata could feel safe — so how could the fisherman's future grandson ever feel secure on the throne? [[mahabharata:adi:100:6]]

Right away, Devavrata promised that the son of the fisherman's daughter would be king. But when the fisherman still looked doubtful, Devavrata made an even bigger promise: "From today, I will never marry, so I will never have children of my own." The fisherman was so amazed and pleased that he agreed at once. The gods themselves rained flowers down from the sky and called out, "This one is Bhishma, the terrible!" because of the terrible vow he had made. [[mahabharata:adi:100:7]]

Bhishma brought the fisherman's daughter home to Hastinapura and told his father everything. Santanu was so moved by his son's sacrifice that he gave Bhishma a wonderful gift: Bhishma would never die until he himself chose to, and death would always wait for his command. [[mahabharata:adi:100:7]]
