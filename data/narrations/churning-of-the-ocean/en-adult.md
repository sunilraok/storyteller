Long ago there stood a mountain named Meru, blazing and golden, the haunt of the gods and Gandharvas, too high for ordinary men even to imagine climbing. [[mahabharata:adi:17:1]] Upon its jewelled peak the gods once sat in conclave, worn from penances and vows, all of them longing for amrita, the nectar of life. [[mahabharata:adi:17:1]] Seeing their anxious mood, Narayana spoke to Brahman: let the gods and the Asuras together churn the Ocean, for from it would come amrita, and every drug and gem besides. [[mahabharata:adi:17:1]]

For a churning rod they chose the mountain Mandara, whose peaks rose like clouds eleven thousand yojanas into the sky and sank as far below. [[mahabharata:adi:18:1]] The gods tried to tear it up themselves but could not, so they went to Vishnu and Brahman and begged them to devise a plan. [[mahabharata:adi:18:1]] Vishnu laid the task on the mighty serpent Ananta, who tore up the mountain, woods, and all its creatures. [[mahabharata:adi:18:1]] Together with Ananta the gods came to the shore of the Ocean and asked leave to churn its waters for nectar; the Ocean agreed, asking only a share of what was found, since it could bear the agitation the mountain would cause. [[mahabharata:adi:18:1]] Then the gods went to the king of the tortoises and asked him to bear the mountain on his back, and he agreed, so Indra set Mandara upon him. [[mahabharata:adi:18:1]]

The gods and Asuras made Mandara their churning staff and the great serpent Vasuki their rope: the Asuras held him by the hood and the gods by the tail, while Ananta stood on the side of the gods, raising and lowering the serpent's hood by turns. [[mahabharata:adi:18:1]] The strain of the churning made Vasuki spew black vapor and flame from his mouth, which turned to rain-clouds and refreshed the tired gods, while flowers fell from the trees on the whirling mountain and refreshed them further. [[mahabharata:adi:18:1]] The Ocean roared like the sea at the end of the world; creatures of the deep were crushed and killed; great trees on the mountain, full of birds, were torn up by the roots and fell into the water, and the friction between them kindled fires that blazed until Indra put them out with rain. [[mahabharata:adi:18:1]]

After the churning had gone on a while, gummy sap from the trees and herbs, carrying the virtue of amrita, mixed into the waters, and the gods who drank that water gained immortality from it, while the churned sea itself thickened by degrees into clarified butter, though nectar still did not appear. [[mahabharata:adi:18:2]] Spent and without strength, the gods went to Brahman and confessed they could churn no further, and Brahman turned to Narayana, who granted them fresh strength to set the mountain back in place and begin again. [[mahabharata:adi:18:2]]

Churning resumed, and from the Ocean rose the gentle Moon of a thousand rays, and after him Lakshmi robed in white, then Soma, and the white steed, and the gem Kaustubha that adorns Narayana's breast. [[mahabharata:adi:18:2]] Then came Dhanwantari himself, carrying the white vessel of nectar, and at the sight of him the Asuras cried out that it should be theirs. [[mahabharata:adi:18:2]] The great elephant Airavata rose next, and Indra claimed him. [[mahabharata:adi:18:2]] But as the churning went on, the poison Kalakuta also rose, blazing and smoking until it stupefied the three worlds with its scent, and Brahman begged Shiva to swallow it for the safety of all creation; Shiva held it in his throat, and from that day he has been called Nilakantha, the blue-throated one. [[mahabharata:adi:18:2]] Seeing the wonders that had emerged, the Asuras despaired and made ready to fight the gods for Lakshmi and the Amrita, so Narayana called on his power of illusion, took the form of a beautiful woman, and beguiled the Danavas until they placed the Amrita in her hands of their own will. [[mahabharata:adi:18:2]]

The Daityas and Danavas then armed themselves and attacked the gods, while Vishnu, still in the form of the enchantress and joined by Nara, deceived the Danavas further and carried the Amrita away from them. [[mahabharata:adi:19:1]] The gods drank it with delight as Vishnu gave it out, but a Danava named Rahu slipped in among them disguised as a god and began to drink too. [[mahabharata:adi:19:1]] Surya and Soma saw through the disguise and told the gods, and just as the Amrita reached Rahu's throat, Narayana cut off his head with his discus. [[mahabharata:adi:19:1]] The severed head, huge as a mountain peak, rose into the sky crying out, while the headless trunk fell and rolled on the earth, making it tremble; and because Rahu's throat had tasted the Amrita, his head lives on and to this day quarrels with Surya and Soma, swallowing them at eclipses. [[mahabharata:adi:19:1]]

Then Narayana put off his female form and turned his weapons on the Danavas, and a fierce battle began on the shore of the salt sea, with javelins, discus-strokes, swords, darts, and maces, until the Asuras lay in their thousands drenched in blood, their gold-adorned heads scattered like red-dyed peaks across the field. [[mahabharata:adi:19:1]] Warriors at a distance struck each other down with iron missiles, and those at close quarters fought with their fists, while the air rang with shouts of "cut," "pierce," "hurl down," "advance." [[mahabharata:adi:19:1]] Then Nara and Narayana themselves entered the field, and Narayana called to mind his discus Sudarsana, bright as fire and dreadful in battle, which came to his hand from the sky and, hurled again and again, destroyed the Daityas and Danavas by the thousand. [[mahabharata:adi:19:1]]

The Danavas rose into the sky and hurled down mountains upon the gods, and the falling peaks collided with a tremendous roar and shook the earth with their forests; but Nara ground those rocks to dust with his gold-headed arrows and filled the heavens with dust of his own. [[mahabharata:adi:19:2]] Beaten back and seeing the discus blazing across the sky, the Danavas fled, some into the bowels of the earth and some into the sea. [[mahabharata:adi:19:2]] Having won the day, the gods set Mandara back upon its own base, and carrying the nectar they had won, they returned rejoicing to heaven, where Indra and the other gods gave the vessel of Amrita into Narayana's keeping. [[mahabharata:adi:19:2]]
