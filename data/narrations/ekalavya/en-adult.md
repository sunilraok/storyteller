Long before he came to teach the Kuru princes, Drona had studied the science of arms under the sage Agnivesa, and his closest companion in those years of discipline was Drupada, prince of the Panchalas. [[mahabharata:adi:133:2]] Drupada loved him as a friend and used to say that when he became king, his kingdom and all its wealth would belong to Drona as much as to himself. [[mahabharata:adi:133:2]] When their studies ended, the two parted, and Drona married Kripi; they had a son, Ashwatthaman, radiant as the sun. [[mahabharata:adi:133:2]]

Drona lived in poverty, and one day his little son Ashwatthaman saw the sons of rich men drinking milk and cried for it. [[mahabharata:adi:133:3]] Drona searched from country to country for a milch cow but found none, and while he was gone the other boys gave Ashwatthaman water mixed with powdered rice; the child, deceived, danced for joy crying that he had drunk milk, while busy-bodies mocked Drona's poverty. [[mahabharata:adi:133:3]] Stung by this, Drona resolved that however he might be censured, he would never serve as anyone's hireling, and instead he took his wife and son and went to claim the promise of his old friend, now installed as king of the Panchalas. [[mahabharata:adi:133:3]]

Drupada did not receive him as a friend. Laughing, he said a great king could have no friendship with so poor and luckless a man, that friendship endures only between equals, and that he did not remember ever promising Drona his kingdom; he offered him only food and shelter for a single night. [[mahabharata:adi:132:1]] [[mahabharata:adi:133:3]] Filled with anger at this insult, Drona left the Panchala capital and turned his steps toward Hastinapura, the city of the Kurus. [[mahabharata:adi:132:1]]

There he lived quietly in the house of Kripa. [[mahabharata:adi:133:1]] One day the young Kuru princes, playing with a ball, let it fall into a well and could not retrieve it; a thin, dark Brahmana passing by smiled at their trouble and offered to bring up both the ball and his own ring, thrown into the well, with nothing but blades of grass, if they would give him a meal. Yudhishthira asked him instead to name a gift that would serve him for life. [[mahabharata:adi:133:1]] Drona pierced the grass blades one through another in a chain and drew up the ball, then took a bow and arrow and drew up the ring the same way, astonishing the princes, who asked who he was and were told to describe him to Bhishma. [[mahabharata:adi:133:2]]

Bhishma recognized Drona at once from their account and went himself to welcome him, bringing him to the palace and asking why he had come. [[mahabharata:adi:133:2]] Drona told him everything — his years with Agnivesa, his friendship with Drupada and the broken promise, the humiliation of his son's mock milk, and Drupada's insult — and said he had come to Hastinapura seeking pupils. [[mahabharata:adi:133:2]] [[mahabharata:adi:133:3]] Bhishma answered that Drona should take up his bow and make the Kuru princes accomplished in arms, and that all the wealth and sovereignty of the Kurus were his to command. [[mahabharata:adi:133:4]]

Drona settled among the Kurus and was given a house filled with grain and wealth, and Bhishma placed his grandsons, the sons of Pandu and of Dhritarashtra, in his care. [[mahabharata:adi:134:1]] Before teaching them, Drona made the princes touch his feet and swear to accomplish a certain purpose once they were skilled in arms; only Arjuna answered, vowing to do it, and Drona embraced him with tears of joy. [[mahabharata:adi:134:1]] Many other princes came to him too, among them Karna, the Suta's adopted son, who from jealousy often defied Arjuna and was upheld in it by Duryodhana; but Arjuna, devoted beyond all the rest to his preceptor and to arms, surpassed every one of his fellow pupils. [[mahabharata:adi:134:1]]

Drona gave each pupil a narrow-mouthed vessel to fill with water, which took long, but gave his own son Ashwatthaman a broad-mouthed one, so as to gain time to teach him alone; Arjuna discovered this and began filling his own vessel at once with the Varuna weapon, so that he lost nothing by it. [[mahabharata:adi:134:2]] Drona also told the cook, in secret, never to feed Arjuna in the dark. One night the wind blew out the lamp while Arjuna was eating, and his hand found his mouth from habit alone; struck by this, he took to practising archery at night. Hearing the twang of his bowstring in the dark, Drona embraced him and promised to make him an archer without equal in the world, and went on to teach him to fight on horse, on elephant, on chariot and on foot, and with mace, sword, lance, spear and dart. [[mahabharata:adi:134:2]]

Among those who came seeking Drona's teaching was Ekalavya, son of Hiranyadhanus, king of the Nishadas; but Drona, mindful of what was proper, refused to accept him, for he was a Nishada who might in time surpass his high-born pupils. [[mahabharata:adi:134:2]] Ekalavya touched Drona's feet and went into the forest, where he made an image of Drona out of clay and worshipped it as his true teacher, practising before it with such discipline that he grew wonderfully skilled in fixing, aiming and loosing his arrows. [[mahabharata:adi:134:2]]

Once, when the Kuru and Pandava princes had gone hunting with a servant and a dog, the dog wandered off and came upon Ekalavya, dark-skinned and matted-haired, and barked at him; Ekalavya, to show his skill, shot seven arrows into its mouth before it could close, and the dog ran back to the princes still pierced by them. [[mahabharata:adi:134:3]] Astonished and ashamed of their own skill, the princes searched the woods and found the archer, who told them he was the son of Hiranyadhanus and a pupil of Drona. [[mahabharata:adi:134:3]] When they reported this to Drona, Arjuna went to him privately and reminded him that he had once promised no pupil would equal him — how then could the Nishada's son surpass him? [[mahabharata:adi:134:3]] Drona thought for a moment, then took Arjuna and went to find Ekalavya, still shooting arrows in his filthy rags. Ekalavya bowed to Drona as his teacher, and Drona said that if he were truly his pupil, he must pay his fee: the thumb of his right hand. [[mahabharata:adi:134:3]]

Ekalavya, devoted to truth and to his own promise, cut off his thumb without hesitation and gave it to Drona; when he tried to shoot again with his remaining fingers, his old swiftness was gone, and Arjuna's jealousy left him. [[mahabharata:adi:134:4]] Among Drona's other pupils, Duryodhana and Bhima excelled with the mace, Ashwatthaman in every weapon, the twins Nakula and Sahadeva with the sword, and Yudhishthira as a charioteer; but Arjuna outdid them all in intelligence, resourcefulness, strength and skill, becoming an Atiratha whose fame reached to the sea, while the sons of Dhritarashtra grew jealous of him and of Bhima. [[mahabharata:adi:134:4]]

At last Drona set an artificial bird atop a tree to test his pupils and called them one by one to aim at it. To Yudhishthira, and then to each of the others in turn, he asked what they saw, and each answered that he saw the tree, himself, his brothers, and the bird; displeased, Drona ordered each of them to stand aside. [[mahabharata:adi:134:5]]
