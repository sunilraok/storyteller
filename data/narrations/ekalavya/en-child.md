Long ago, a poor Brahmana named Drona had a friend named Drupada, a prince of the Panchalas. They had studied together, and Drupada once promised that when he became king, he would share his kingdom with Drona. [[mahabharata:adi:133:2]]

Years passed. Drona married a woman named Kripi, and they had a son named Ashwatthaman. [[mahabharata:adi:133:2]] The family was poor. One day little Ashwatthaman saw rich boys drinking milk and cried because he wanted some too. [[mahabharata:adi:133:3]] Drona could not find a cow to give him milk, and some boys tricked Ashwatthaman with rice water instead, and people laughed at how poor Drona was. [[mahabharata:adi:133:3]] Drona felt so ashamed that he decided to go and ask his old friend Drupada for help, remembering his promise. [[mahabharata:adi:133:3]]

But when Drona reached him, Drupada would not remember their friendship. He said kings cannot be friends with poor men, and he only offered Drona food and a place to sleep for one night. [[mahabharata:adi:132:1]] [[mahabharata:adi:133:3]] Hurt and angry, Drona left and travelled to the city of Hastinapura instead. [[mahabharata:adi:132:1]]

There, Drona lived quietly in the house of a man named Kripa. [[mahabharata:adi:133:1]] One day some princes lost their ball down a well, and Drona helped them get it back using nothing but blades of grass, and then he shot an arrow to bring up his own ring too. [[mahabharata:adi:133:1]] [[mahabharata:adi:133:2]] The princes were amazed and told Bhishma about it, who welcomed Drona warmly and asked him to become the princes' teacher. [[mahabharata:adi:133:2]] [[mahabharata:adi:133:4]] Bhishma was the grandfather who watched over all the Kuru princes. [[mahabharata:adi:134:1]]

Drona agreed, and taught all the princes archery and how to fight. [[mahabharata:adi:134:1]] He noticed that Arjuna worked harder than anyone else, practising even at night once the lamp blew out, and Drona promised to make Arjuna the finest archer in the world. [[mahabharata:adi:134:2]]

One day, a boy named Ekalavya came and asked Drona to teach him too. But Drona said no, because Ekalavya was a Nishada, and Drona would not accept him as a pupil. [[mahabharata:adi:134:2]] Ekalavya was not angry. He went into the forest, made a clay statue of Drona, and practised archery in front of it every day as if Drona were really his teacher. [[mahabharata:adi:134:2]] He became a wonderful archer all on his own.

One day a dog from the princes' hunting party found Ekalavya in the forest and barked at him. Ekalavya, without hurting the dog, shot seven arrows into its open mouth so quickly that it ran back to the princes still holding them. [[mahabharata:adi:134:3]] Everyone was amazed, and they went looking for the archer who had done it. When they found Ekalavya, he told them he was a pupil of Drona. [[mahabharata:adi:134:3]]

Arjuna was troubled by this, because Drona had once promised that no pupil would be greater than him. [[mahabharata:adi:134:3]] So Drona went with Arjuna to find Ekalavya. When Ekalavya saw his teacher, he bowed to him happily. Drona told him that if he were truly his pupil, he must pay a fee: the thumb of his right hand. [[mahabharata:adi:134:3]]

Ekalavya, who always kept his promises, cut off his thumb at once and gave it to Drona, without any anger in his heart. [[mahabharata:adi:134:4]] After that, he could never shoot quite as fast as before. [[mahabharata:adi:134:4]]

Among all of Drona's students, each one became skilled in a different way — some with the mace, some with the sword, some driving chariots — but Arjuna became the very best of them all. [[mahabharata:adi:134:4]]
