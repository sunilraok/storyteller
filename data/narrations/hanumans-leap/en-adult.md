Hanuman resolved to follow the path of the sky-going Charanas in search of the place where Ravana had carried Sita away. [[ramayana:sundara:1:1]] The task was his alone, with no companion to share it, and he set about it on Mount Mahendra, frightening birds, crushing trees against his chest, and ranging the mountain's lapis-hued lawns like a lion bursting with strength. [[ramayana:sundara:1:1]] The mountain was crowded with Nagas and veined with metals of many colors, and Yakshas, Kinnaras, Gandharvas and Pannagas dwelt upon it in splendid array; Hanuman stood among them like a great serpent in a lake. [[ramayana:sundara:1:1]]

Joining his palms, he saluted the Sun, Indra, the Wind, the Self-create Brahma, and all beings, and set his heart upon the journey. [[ramayana:sundara:1:1]] Facing east, he bowed to his father the Wind, then turned south and began to grow in size for the crossing, swelling as the sea swells at the full moon, glad at heart, growing among the monkeys for Rama's sake. [[ramayana:sundara:1:1]] Wishing to cross the ocean, he pressed the mountain with his arms and legs; it shook, and blossoms showered from the trees until the whole mountain wore a floral look. Water burst from it like musth from an elephant, and its veins of gold, silver and collyrium showed forth, and crags of red arsenic broke open. [[ramayana:sundara:1:1]] Snakes in the mountain's caves bit at the rocks in rage and spat fire, and all the creatures dwelling within cried out in fear, filling the earth and the sky with their voices; ascetics and Vidyadharas, thinking the mountain was being riven apart, fled into the air, abandoning their golden vessels and garlands and food. [[ramayana:sundara:1:1]] But the ascetics and Charanas stationed in the sky understood: this was Hanuman, son of the Wind, undertaking a hard task for Rama and the monkeys, wishing to cross the sea to its farther shore. [[ramayana:sundara:1:1]]

Then Hanuman roared like a thundercloud, lashed his tail, planted his mighty arms upon the mountain, drew in his waist and legs, and gathered his strength of body and mind. Fixing his eyes on the sky, he held his breath, and spoke to the monkeys gathered there: "As an arrow shot by Rama courses like the wind, so will I course to Lanka, ruled by Ravana. If I do not find Janaka's daughter in Lanka, I shall go with the same speed to the realm of the gods. And if I still do not find Sita, I shall bring the Rakshasa king back bound. Either I shall return having succeeded, or I shall uproot Lanka itself, with Ravana in it, and carry it here." [[ramayana:sundara:1:1]] So saying, without further thought, he sprang up, deeming himself like Garuda. The trees of the mountain, caught in the rush of his leap, tore free of the ground and followed him a while through the sky, like friends escorting a friend setting out on a distant journey, before falling away into the sea below. [[ramayana:sundara:1:1]] Covered in fragrant flowers shaken loose by his flight, his shadow lying ten yojanas wide and thirty long upon the water, he flew on; the Sun did not burn him and the Wind served him, for the sake of Rama's errand, and sages sang his praises, while gods, Gandharvas and Danavas scattered blossoms upon him as he passed. [[ramayana:sundara:1:1]]

The Ocean, remembering that it had been reared by Sagara of the Ikshvaku race, Rama's own line, resolved that it would be wrong to let this monkey tire without aid. [[ramayana:sundara:1:1]] So it commanded the golden mountain Mainaka, who lay hidden beneath its waters, to rise and offer Hanuman a place to rest. Mainaka rose at once, its golden peaks splitting the sky like a second sunrise. [[ramayana:sundara:1:1]] Hanuman, taking the risen mountain for an obstacle in his path, struck it down with his chest as he flew — until Mainaka, taking the form of a man, stood on its own summit and called to him, explaining that in ancient days it had possessed wings, until Indra sheared the wings from all the mountains in his wrath; Mainaka alone had been saved when the Wind cast it into this very ocean and hid it from Indra's sight. Now, wishing to repay that old kindness to the Wind's son, it begged Hanuman to rest a while and eat of its fruits before going on. [[ramayana:sundara:1:1]] Hanuman answered kindly that he was pleased by the welcome, but that his errand pressed him and he had vowed not to pause anywhere on the way; he touched the mountain with his hand in courtesy and flew smiling on, while the Ocean and Mainaka looked after him with blessing, and Indra himself, well pleased, praised Mainaka for its help to Rama's cause. [[ramayana:sundara:1:1]]

Then the gods, wishing to test Hanuman's strength, asked Surasa, mother of the serpents, to bar his way. Taking a huge and terrible shape with a gaping mouth, she told him that the gods had ordained him as her food, and that he must enter her mouth. Hanuman told her plainly who he was and on what errand he went — envoy of Rama, son of Dasharatha, seeking Sita who had been carried off by Ravana — and asked her to let him pass, or else let him first find Sita and return before being eaten; but Surasa answered that none might pass her without being devoured, for such was her boon. [[ramayana:sundara:1:1]] So Hanuman grew larger to match her mouth: as she widened it to ten yojanas, he grew to ten; as she stretched it to twenty, he grew to twenty, and so on, each matching the other, until her mouth measured a hundred yojanas across. Then, in an instant, Hanuman shrank himself to the size of a thumb, darted into her mouth, and sprang out again before she could close it. Bowing to her, he told her that he had indeed entered her mouth as she required, and that her boon was fulfilled. Delighted, Surasa resumed her own gentle form and blessed his errand, and all beings who witnessed this third of Hanuman's feats praised him, crying "Excellent! Excellent!" [[ramayana:sundara:1:1]]

Flying on, he was seen by the Rakshasi Sinhika, who was accustomed to seizing her prey by its shadow. She caught hold of Hanuman's shadow, and he felt his flight suddenly checked, as a ship is held back by a contrary wind. Looking about, he saw the strange creature that had risen from the sea and understood that this must be Sinhika. He made his body vast to match her, and she opened her mouth wide enough to swallow the sky itself and rushed at him; but Hanuman shrank himself in an instant and let himself be drawn into her mouth, then tore at her vitals with his sharp claws until her heart was torn out and she fell dead into the sea. The beings ranging the sky, who had watched, praised the deed and urged him on to the end of his errand. [[ramayana:sundara:1:1]]

At last, near the end of a hundred yojanas, he saw a wooded shore and an island covered with the trees of the Malaya hills. He surveyed the sea and the land beyond it, and, thinking that the Rakshasas would be roused to curiosity if they saw him still in his vast form, he shrank himself back to his natural size before he reached the mountain on the farther shore. [[ramayana:sundara:1:1]] There he alighted, and looking up from the shore of the great ocean, he beheld Lanka set upon the mountain-top before him, as splendid as Amaravati. [[ramayana:sundara:1:1]]
